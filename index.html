Before you begin

Better outcomes, not magic words.

This workshop isn't about memorizing prompt formulas. It's about knowing what you want, giving AI what it needs, and noticing when it takes you somewhere else.

Setup

Get ready

  1. Open chatgpt.com in a new tab and sign in. A free account works for everything here.
  2. Keep this page open beside it. You'll move back and forth as you work.
  3. Think of one real thing you'd like help finishing: an email, a plan, a schedule, a document, a decision. You'll choose it in chapter 04.

How the prompts work

Every prompt on this page shows where it goes:

Starts a new chat. Open in ChatGPT fills it in for you.Paste into the same chat you're already working in.Attach the practice files first, then paste.
One rule. Don't paste anything confidential into ChatGPT: personal information, student or customer records, passwords, or internal documents you haven't been cleared to share. If your task involves that kind of material, describe it instead of pasting it.

Part one: How it works

Create a bowl of fruit.

One identical prompt, a room full of screens, and a lesson about who is really making the decisions.

Exercise 1.1

Round one

  1. Click Open in ChatGPT. Don't add a word.
  2. Compare your image with the people around you.
Create an image of a bowl of fruit.

What did you decide?

For each choice, mark who actually made it.

AI fills in what you leave blank.

The risk of outsourcing decisions.

The images in this room aren't identical, but they probably aren't very different either. Most are safe, polished versions of the same default.

That's fine for a bowl of fruit. Now picture a proposal, a cover letter, or a lesson plan, with millions of people letting the same system make the same choices. Everyone's work starts to look the same.

Talk about it

  • Who chose the bowl? The fruit? The lighting, background, and style?
  • Which of those decisions actually mattered to you?
Exercise 1.2

Keep the decisions that matter

Pick a purpose. Then decide no more than three things that purpose depends on, and leave the rest to the AI on purpose.

Talk about it

  • Compare with someone who picked a different purpose. How much did three choices change?
  • Which decision did the most work?

Remember

  • AI fills in what you leave blank.
  • The point isn't more detail. It's keeping the decisions that matter, and handing off the rest on purpose.

Part one: How it works

What ChatGPT is actually doing.

You don't need the math. You need to know enough about how it works to predict how it will behave.

  • 1

    It predicts. It doesn't know.

    A large language model writes the most likely next words, based on patterns from an enormous amount of text. Most likely isn't the same as true, and it isn't the same as yours.

    SoLeave it blanks and you get the average. That's your bowl of fruit.
  • 2

    Every answer is a new draw.

    Ask the same thing twice and you'll get a different answer. There's no single "right" response waiting inside it.

    SoDon't treat the first answer as the answer. Ask for options.
  • 3

    It only works with what's in front of it.

    The conversation, the files you attach, search results, and anything saved in its memory. It doesn't know your job, your audience, or what you decided last week unless you tell it.

    SoContext is the biggest lever you have. Chapter 05.
  • 4

    Long conversations get crowded.

    Everything said so far shapes the next answer. As a chat grows, early decisions carry less weight, and eventually it runs out of room.

    SoWhen things feel off, start fresh. Chapter 03.
  • 5

    It's built to keep going.

    It's tuned to be helpful and agreeable. It rarely says "I'm not sure" or "this isn't what you asked for" unless you ask, and it can make a wrong turn sound reasonable.

    SoYou have to own the destination. It won't.
  • 6

    It can use tools.

    Search the web, read files, analyze spreadsheets, make images, listen to your voice. Tools change what it can see in the moment.

    SoPick the right tool for each stage. Chapter 07.
Exercise 2.1

See it for yourself

  2. Then ask it to show the decisions it made for you:

Remember

  • It predicts. It doesn't know.
  • Every answer is a new draw.
  • It only works with what's in front of it.

Part one: How it works

AI is not a GPS.

A GPS reroutes you when you miss a turn, but it never changes your destination. AI can change both, then explain why the new destination was better all along.

Drift is easy to see in a picture, so let's watch it happen.

Exercise 3.1

Five small edits

  1. Go back to your bowl-of-fruit chat from exercise 1.2.
  2. Ask for five edits, one at a time, each changing one thing only. Tap an idea to copy it, or write your own.
  3. After each edit, write down anything else that changed that you didn't ask for.
What I asked to changeWhat else changed anyway

AI can change the destination one reasonable-looking step at a time.

Each edit is a brand-new image guided by everything said so far, so small unrequested changes stack up. The same thing happens in an email, a plan, or a spreadsheet. It's just harder to see.

When things seem off, start a new conversation.

  • It brings back a mistake you already fixed
  • It forgets something you decided earlier
  • It changes things you didn't ask about
  • Answers get longer, vaguer, or more generic
  • You're arguing with it instead of building
  • You can't remember what you agreed on either
Exercise 3.2

Start fresh without losing your work

Send this in your bowl chat. Then open a new chat, paste the summary, and ask for the image again. Is it closer to what you meant than edit five?

Summarize what we've decided so far: the goal, the decisions we've locked in, the context that matters, and what's still open. Write it so I can paste it into a brand-new conversation and pick up exactly where we left off.

You don't need to know the route. You do need to know the destination.

One great question, or twenty ordinary ones.

Both can get you to the same place, as long as the destination doesn't move. The skill isn't perfect wording. It's knowing where you're going well enough to notice when the AI isn't taking you there.

Remember

  • AI can change the destination and make it sound reasonable.
  • When it feels off, start fresh and carry your decisions forward.

Part two: Best practices

What does "finished" look like?

Before you think about wording, decide what done looks like. This is where your real task begins.

A vague request can still work if you know where you're going. A polished prompt aimed at a fuzzy destination gets you a polished wrong answer.

Vague"Help me with onboarding."
Clear destination"When this is finished, our shift lead can run a new hire's first week from one page."
Exercise 4.1

Define your destination

Choose something you actually need to finish. You'll keep working on it through the rest of the workshop.

Remember

  • You don't need the route. You need the destination.
  • Write down what finished looks like before you write a prompt.
05

Part two: Best practices

Context.

The biggest lever you have isn't clever wording. It's what the AI knows when it starts working.

Prompt engineering vs. context engineering.

Prompt engineering asks"What words should I use?"
Context engineering asks"What does the AI need to know to help me succeed?"

What counts as context?

01Goal and success criteria
02Audience and purpose
03Source documents and evidence
04Examples of good or bad output
05Constraints and non-negotiables
06History and previous decisions
07What's already been tried
08Policies or rules
09Tone, format, and where it's used

Every blank becomes a decision.

You can't fill in every blank, and you shouldn't. Context is how you choose which decisions to keep and which to hand over on purpose.

Exercise 5.1

Same task, different context

Flat Cap Co. makes camouflage flat caps for the whole family, pets included. They're changing their return policy.

Round one

Write an announcement about a policy change.

Round two

Choose who it's for. The prompt below already includes the memo.

Memo from Felix Bramble, Operations. November 2, 2026.

Returns are changing on December 1. Every return will get one of five reason codes: Fit, Color or pattern, Damaged, Wrong item, Changed mind. Support staff choose the code. Customers no longer type in their own reason.

Why: First-quarter returns rose 13%. "Fit not as expected" was the top reason at 38%, but reasons were logged so inconsistently that we couldn't tell what to fix.

What's new for customers: Returns for fit become a free exchange for a different size within 30 days, and we pay shipping both ways. All other returns stay refund-only with a $6 return label fee.

Staff action: Finish the 10-minute reason-code guide by November 24. Questions go to Lindsay Draper.

Talk about it

  • What did round one make up?
  • Compare with someone who chose the other audience. Same facts, how different is it?
  • Which single piece of context changed the result the most?

Specificity beats volume.

A long prompt isn't automatically a good one. A useful detail rules out wrong answers. Irrelevant detail is just noise.

Exercise 5.2

Context for your task

Check what the AI would need to know about your task. Add a quick note for each one you check, and skip anything that wouldn't change the result.

Let it interview you

Not sure what you're missing? This opens a new chat with your task filled in. Keep that chat open for the next chapter.

Remember

  • Ask what the AI needs to know, not which words to use.
  • Specificity beats volume.

Part two: Best practices

Stop trying to write the perfect prompt.

Most good results come from a conversation: start with the problem, react, add what's missing, reject what's wrong, keep what's good.

Conversation is not failure.

Needing ten prompts doesn't mean you did it wrong. The middle turns are often where you find out what the real problem is and what the result needs to be.

    07

    Part three: Tools and workflows

    Some problems are bigger than one prompt.

    Once a task takes more than one exchange, stop treating it as a prompt problem. Break it into stages, and give each stage the right context and the right tool.

    Think in stages.

    UnderstandGatherEvaluatePlanCreateReviewReviseDeliver

    Different stages need different context, and different tools.

    Gathering needs your sources. Evaluating needs data. Creating needs examples and tone. Reviewing needs the goal. ChatGPT has a tool for each. Pick a station and try it.

    Files

    For gathering. Your source material becomes its working environment.

    Meeting notesMeeting transcript
    Using only these two files, list every action item with its owner and due date. After each one, quote the line it came from. Then tell me anything the two files disagree on.

    Check itOpen the meeting notes and confirm two of the action items yourself.

    Data analysis

    For evaluating. It runs real calculations on your spreadsheet.

    Returns spreadsheet
    Analyze this returns spreadsheet. Show the number of returns by reason as a table and a bar chart. A meeting summary claimed "fit not as expected" was the top return reason at 38%. Does this data support that? Tell me what you'd check before trusting either one.

    Check itDid it notice the Warehouse Notes column is nonsense, and that this data doesn't back up the meeting's claim?

    Web search

    For gathering current facts it couldn't know on its own.

    Check itOpen one link. Does the page actually say what ChatGPT said it does?

    Voice

    For understanding. Talking is often the fastest way to get context out of your head.

    In the ChatGPT phone app, tap the voice button and explain your task out loud for two minutes, the way you'd tell a coworker. Then say:

    Turn what I just said into a clear list: my goal, my constraints, the decisions I've made, and the questions I still need to answer.

    Check itDid it capture what you meant, or fill in gaps you never mentioned?

    Images were chapter 01. Features change over time. Choosing the right capability for the stage doesn't.

    AI doesn't have to do every step.

    Good jobs for AI

    • First drafts and rewrites
    • Summarizing sources
    • Generating options
    • Reformatting and organizing
    • First-pass analysis
    • Critique against your goal

    Stays with you

    • The destination
    • Priorities and trade-offs
    • Approvals
    • Ethical calls
    • Checking facts you'll stand behind
    • Responsibility for the result

    Start at the end.

    For bigger work, begin with "what does finished look like?" and design backward. What has to exist right before the finished product? What information does that need, and where does it come from? What should AI do, and what should you decide?

    Exercise 7.1

    Reverse-design your workflow

    Finished

    Work backward. Each stage you add goes before the ones you already have. Three or four stages is plenty.

    A prompt is a component, not the system.

    The prompt is one instruction inside a workflow. The AI is one part of a system made of people, tools, and checks. You keep the destination, the approvals, and the responsibility.

    Remember

    • Different stages need different context and tools.
    • AI doesn't have to do every step.
    • Design backward from finished.
    08

    Part four: Your prompt

    Build something real.

    The goal isn't to write a good prompt. It's to make real progress on something you need.

    Exercise 8.1

    Your starting prompt

    Built from everything you've entered: your destination, your decisions, your context, and your stages.

    1. Open it in a new chat. Attach source files if you have them, after checking the one rule.
    2. Work through your first stage. Steer. Keep what's good.
    3. If the conversation starts to feel off, use the start-fresh prompt from chapter 03.

    Before anything leaves your screen.

    • Is it still headed to your destination, or did it move?
    • Check every name, number, date, and source.
    • Remove anything it made up or can't support.
    • You read it. You sign off. Your name is on it, not the AI's.
    09

    Part four: Your prompt

    Now write the perfect prompt.

    The "perfect prompt" is easy to write once you've explored the problem and know where you're going. So let the AI write it.

    Exercise 9.1

    Turn your conversation into a high-impact prompt

    In the chat where you built your task, send this:

    Review everything we did in this conversation. Write the single prompt that could have produced something close to our final result from the beginning. Then explain which parts of that prompt mattered most and why.

    Then test it

    Paste that prompt into a new chat. If it gets close, you have a reusable prompt. If it doesn't, you've just seen how much the conversation was doing.

    Remember

    • The perfect prompt is easiest to write at the end.
    • Test it in a fresh chat before you trust it.

    Your prompt toolkit.

    The prompts worth keeping, all in one place. Bookmark this page and come back whenever you start something new.

    Your starting prompt

    Built from your plan. Use it to restart your task any time.

    Let it interview you

    When you're not sure what context matters.

    I need help with this: [describe your task]. When this is finished, [who] will be able to [what]. Before you write anything, ask me the five questions whose answers would most change the result. Ask one at a time and wait for my answer.

    Surface hidden decisions

    When the answer feels generic.

    What assumptions are you making? List every decision you made for me that I didn't specify.

    Escape the first default

    When you want real options, not variations.

    Give me three genuinely different approaches. For each, tell me what it's best for and what it gives up.

    Check the route

    Before you call anything finished.

    Critique this against the goal we established. What would still make it fail?

    Start fresh

    When the conversation starts to feel off.

    Summarize what we've decided so far: the goal, the decisions we've locked in, the context that matters, and what's still open. Write it so I can paste it into a brand-new conversation and pick up exactly where we left off.

    Stick to my sources

    When you're working from your own documents.

    Using only the files I've attached, answer the following. After each point, quote the line it came from. If the files don't say, tell me instead of guessing.

    Teach it back

    After any conversation that worked.

    Review everything we did in this conversation. Write the single prompt that could have produced something close to our final result from the beginning. Then explain which parts of that prompt mattered most and why.
